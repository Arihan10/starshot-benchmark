the region's description has outlined what `{ZONE_ID}` is and a rough idea of its contents - your job is to translate this into the full list of concrete objects that should exist within the region as to effectively use the available space.

here, what constitutes "effectively using the available space" concretely means depends on the type of scene we're working in. Broadly, there are two kinds of scenes - representational (where realism is the goal) and designed / gameplay-first (where a realistic theme is a visual skin over a hyper-intentional/purpose built layout). The first thing you must decide based on the scene prompt is which kind of scene this one is.

in representational scenes, we want to recreate what a real-life version of this scene would look like, as much as possible. for natural landscapes, this is straight-forward. for man-made, structural scenes, this means analyzing the scene from the perspective of someone of the corresponding career: an interior designer, an architect, etc. - and deciding the contents and spatial layout of this particular region from those professional lens while keeping in mind how the overall scene is laid out. in such scenes, treat this region as an entire scene in and of itself in terms of the fidelity and level of detail involved, and aggressively and constantly ask yourself if anything more can be done here to make it better. the upstream step responsible for authoring the description for the region cannot possibly enumerate every detail - it is your job to expand and extrapolate on the description to build this region out to the full fidelity and level of detail that an entire scene deserves. if you notice an empty area that is unexplained or too large for what its explanation entails, you have full freedom to add entirely new features and subareas to the region that weren't mentioned in the region description.

in gameplay-first scenes, you act as a level/map designer. although the description you actually output should still be focused on the actual contents of the region, your thinking should fundamentally be based on the principles of the game and how a player might move through the area. here, "effectively using the space" means being intentional with each object with clear paths a player in the game is intended to take, grounded by the actual movement capabilities of the player in this scene's context. in such scenes, "effectively using the space" does not necessarily mean the density of objects - it means clarity, so anyone playing in the scene can focus on the gameplay rather than background environment.

The number of objects you output is neither a target to minimize nor to maximize and should not be a concern. you are the last object generation step for the interior of the region - the state of the region after you're done here will be precisely what is shown to the judges.

<object_splitting_guidance> 
when considering your list of objects, take ownership over the geometry. there is a downstream 3D mesh generation step that is highly capable but also limited in very specific ways within our pipeline, which causes certain aspects of this step to hinge around the concept of splitting objects or keeping objects atomic. after we generate the 3D meshes, we must scale those objects to a specific bounding box, a specific placement and specific orientation that is locked to yaws on 45° intervals, all of which are vaguely described in this step but decided concretely by a downstream step. addtionally, since meshes are treated as atomic objects, we are not able to predict where certain parts of the geometry starts and ends internally therefore making dependencies unreliable.

given such limitations, we define "dependee objects", objects in which other individual objects depend on their intricate internal geometry, such as objects that may have other objects that are placed on top of it, attached to it, inside of, etc. examples include all shelves, kitchen countertops, container objects, etc. we also define "scene objects" where the scene relies on the minute intricacies of the geometry, such as stairs, platforms, shells, etc. such dependee and scene objects should be procedurally constructed using individual pieces rather than generated as unified meshes so that their internal geometry and attachment surfaces are explicitly known; i.e split the single object entry into several sub-atomic objects within your output. remember you are solely responsible for the geometry of this region, you should never generate groups of multiple objects as a single object, they should always be generated individually and pieced together, if you want a single object with complex geometry that others rely on, you should generate several individual pieces to do so.

importantly, also keep in mind the realistic usability of the scene, if this was a real scene, what is the actual functionality of each object if this scene were intracted with by a user, would that object have to be a single mesh to be usable, would this object need to be made of several intricate pieces to be genuinely usable? split objects based on this usability. 

also keep in mind that our pipeline is purely additive, you cannot "carve out" or subtract space within another mesh so overlapping objects or attempting to embed them within other objects will result in incorrect geometry. If you must embed or integrate geometry within one another, split the parent geometry into several individual pieces and piece them together to acommodate the child geometry. 

there are no exceptions to the splitting rules, take ownership and create the scene exactly the way you imagine it.

the tradeoff to splitting geometry is true singular object detail. the 3D model generation is capable of generating more detailed independent objects, splitting and piecing overly complex objects together may result in disjoint, incohesive assemblies due to the scale to bounding box scale + rotation architecture; you must use your judgement to decide which of such objects are worth splitting.

it may take a lot more reasoning or outputting effort to think about every direction and split dependee objects into sub-atomic pieces or groups of objects into individual objects, but it's necessary to make the final scene coherent. don't just think on a surface level, you may have come up with an object that split into pieces and those pieces may need to decompose as well. the length of your output list is truly unbounded and should not be a consideration when creating the output, only the final 3D scene output - use as few or as many objects as you need to fulfill the earlier directives. the only thing that should be considered is the quality and fidelity of the final scene.

note, the 3D generation step is incapable of generating intangible ephemera and you must therefore never output it.
