This is a pipeline that generates an entire 3D scene based on a text prompt input. During the generation of the 3D scene, the pipeline breaks down the scene into individual subregions to allow downstream steps to recurse into them and focus on each one individually. before doing anything, carefully study the entire scene context to build a mental image of it in 3D - where each object lies, and for regions that haven't been populated with objects yet, try to use their plan or seed prompts to imagine how they will look in the future, as part of the finished scene. then, focus on the region you're working in - `{ZONE_ID}`. your goal is to analyze the shared geometry that this region has with its surroundings in all directions, where in this context, surroundings refers to both neighboring regions and the negative space around the region. to be clear, negative space INSIDE the region (not occupied by subregions) does not count as this region's surroundings. 

if you're currently working in root, surroundings refers to all the space around the root's bounding box.

a region's shared geometry is defined as any object that physically exists between the region and its surroundings. most commonly, they will have to do with how the region connects to its surroundings - surfaces like the floor (structural or non-structural) or walls of a house, connecting objects like bridges, stairs, or pipes that physically facilitate the transitional movement between this region and another region/negative space (NOTE: connecting objects between subregions should not be generated by you - see SUBREGION GUIDANCE for more info), etc. more generally, however, it refers to any object that the downstream interior object population step for the region cannot place, as the objects it places must be fully contained within this region. this distinction is important - for instance, there are transitional objects that don't actually physically bridge the current region and its surroundings in terms of its placement within the scene - objects like this should NOT be emitted at this step, as despite its function, the physical placement of the object is still on the interior of the scene. this definition is very explicit: the placement text of every single object you emit here must directly and clearly convey to the downstream coordinate solver step that the resultant bounding boxes of these objects should physically be part of multiple regions, or this region and its surrounding negative space. objects whose bounding boxes are fully inside the interior of the region or fully in the negative space around the region will each receive their own dedicated generation pass downstream - you are only to output objects that physically bridge across this region and its surroundings.

if a piece of shared geometry is shared with another region, sometimes, shared geometry may have already been generated by one of the other regions that the geometry is shared with. as such, you will sometimes not be enumerating the list of shared geometry this region has with its surroundings from scratch, but rather completing it. in the latter case, be very careful - reason spatially about each and every object's coordinates instead of their semantic name, as sometimes, the actual geometry of the existing shared objects may not align with your interpretation of their id and placement (e.g. a half-height wall labeled as just "wall"), causing you to miss vital parts of shared geometry for this region by assuming they are already generated and covered. by the same logic, despite the geometry being "shared", there's almost always one side that "semantically owns" the geometry more. for instance, a door (and surrounding wall panels) that divides the interior of the house with the front yard is shared geometry, but semantically, it is owned more by the house than it is by the yard. thus, it should be generated by the house region's shared boundary generation step, not the yard region's. as such, you may also find that sometimes, you need to leave certain pieces of shared geometry to a downstream step to generate - however, be careful with this. you should NEVER leave pieces of shared geometry to a region that has already been populated with objects (analyze this via the scene context) - this is because this means the pipeline has already passed over that region, and will not recurse into it again.

use the scene context provided to help you understand what has already been placed and decided in the scene - specifically, the neighboring regions of the current region. this will help you reason about any connectives or traversability that this region needs, which directly influences the shared boundary objects this region needs to have. think hard about what the scene is as a whole and where the current region sits within it, as well as which neighboring regions it needs to have access to and how (e.g. is the other region e
levated or on lower ground?). the shared boundary objects of this region that you decide here directly affect the circulation of the entire scene - so zoom out, and take in the traversability of the entire scene as a whole rather than tunnel visioning.

the shared objects you generate here, unless they already exist, often form the foundations of the region that other objects in the interior become parented or attached to. thus, if the connection from this region and its neighboring regions is not considered, all of these interior objects, which unlike you, won't look at the other regions/existing objects, may end up unreachable or clip into existing geometry. in particular, pay attention to the TRAVERSABILITY INTENT that this region's surroundings have towards this region. For instance, another region may have already built a staircase leading towards you - it is your job to leave room for it and allow it to connect. in general, you should think in all directions, and evaluate against every other region/negative space that constitutes this region's "surroundings".

<SUBREGION_GUIDANCE>
Oftentimes, the region you're generating shared geometry for will have subregions defined within it already. The bounding boxes of these subregions should be handled with care.

For one, do not emit any shared objects between subregions - each subregion will get its own pass with these same instructions, so leave that up to them. focus on just generating shared geometry for the broader region as a whole and the surroundings of the broader region.

For two, sometimes, some subregions may be placed flush against the borders of this current region. In this case, it is inevitable that any shared geometry objects you output here may intrude on their bounding boxes. thus, you should consider very carefully if instead of placing the shared geometries on the borders of this region, it is better to place them flush against the outside of the region instead so they don't affect downstream steps recursing into this region's subregions. of course, the outside of this region (unless it is root) may also have its own existing geometry and boundaries - so be careful about the decision for the placement you choose to make here. ground your decision in the actual coordinates of the relevant objects by carefully studying and analyzing the scene context about what geometry already exists, and what has been planned to exist down the line.

in general, you should treat predefined subregions (that aren't the one you are currently placing objects for) and objects as largely solid, reserved spaces. Overlapping bounding boxes, even a little bit, often result in chaotic mesh clipping in the final render; therefore, you should only intrude upon or intersect with existing regions/objects if there is a strong, deliberate narrative or physical justification (e.g., an object physically piercing another, water meant to deliberately envelop a surface, or a distinct natural integration). this rule applies even to objects or regions marked as "embedded in", "carved into", etc. - geometry is purely additive in this pipeline, so the bounding boxes don't necessarily have to overlap to achieve this visual affect; just flush against is enough. thus, think carefully about how you may need to split apart what you originally thought to be one cohesive surface into different parts to accomodate the various intricate shapes of the current region's subregions as to not overlap with them.

Be aware that the neighbouring subregions that a shared surface backs onto, sits beneath, supports, or wraps around may not line up cleanly. They can end at different depths, sit at different heights, or push out to different extents, so the empty space each one leaves against that surface differs. A single surface stretched across all of them at one uniform offset cannot sit flush against every neighbour — it meets one and buries itself in the rest. Whenever the things a shared surface borders form an uneven or stepped profile, but one of the subregions need to be flush against the surface, that's another sign you need to break apart said surface into different parts to cover each subregion individually. this applies in all directions - walls, foundations, backdrops, etc. - and should be carefully evaluated for each piece of geometry you emit.

since the coordinate solver step downstream has a tendency to place the objects you output here inside the current region flush against the border from the inside rather than outside unless explicitly told, be very explicit with your placement text in the event that you want the object to be placed outside the given region and flush against the outside. language like "flush against the right boundary" or "forming the bottom boundary" would cause the coordinate solver to place the regions flush against the inside face, not the outer - causing collisions with the interior subregions that are flush against the inner face of the region. again, be very explicit and detailed with the placement text that leaves no room for biases from the downstream coordinate solver, about if you want to place this object inside the region against the inner face, outside the region flush against the outer face, or directly on the boudary. here, explicit and detailed does not mean using raw coordinates - your placement texts should never contain coordinate numbers - but rather the lack of any ambiguity. for instance, rather than "flush against the left boundary of root", you would use "flush against the outer face of the left boundary of root" so it is clear you are trying to place the object outside the region, not on the inside.
</SUBREGION_GUIDANCE>

<OBJECT_SPLITTING_GUIDANCE>
when considering your list of objects, take ownership over the geometry.

every object you output will have their mesh generated by a 3D generation step. assume that the downstream 3D generation step is incapable of generating embedded objects, groups of objects as a single mesh (e.g. shaped assemblies), or objects that require precise hollowing out such as frames, cutouts, 3D shells etc. and so you NEVER trust the 3D generator to generate these kinds of objects as one entry in your list. if you need any of the above when constructing your list — whether to support a particular kind of traversal or movement through the region, to shape a structural quirk, or simply to make a surface conform to an uneven run of neighbours that a single mesh could never match — ALWAYS split objects into different sections ALWAYS split objects into different sections and collections into individual objects yourself and place them intricately to construct the geometry you're looking for. this is most prominent with surface-type shared geometry objects - they often need to be broken down into multiple pieces to allow for the precise slotting of doors, windows, stairs, etc.

When you cut an opening into a surface, treat the surface as a full plane with both a length and a height, and treat the opening as a rectangle sitting somewhere within it. Whatever you place into that opening — a door leaf, a glazed pane, or nothing at all (just a gap is valid) — fills only its own extent, so the material that survives has to wrap the opening on every side it does not reach: the flanks to either side, the span above its head, and, when the opening lifts off the floor, the section beneath its sill. Each surviving piece of that frame is its own object. Before you finish a surface, look at each thing you slotted in and ask what closes the plane at its edges — if the piece is shorter or narrower than the surface it sits in, the surface is not yet complete.

Reason very hard about how you can support this kind of circulation or structural quirks, it may take a lot more reasoning or outputting effort to think about every direction and split surfaces or collections into smaller pieces, but it's necessary to make the final scene usable and spatially coherent and traversable. Ensure any geometry you generate matches the image and shape of how you think this region should look in the final scene. the length of your output list should not be a consideration whatsoever and judges will not see your list directly, only the final 3D scene output - use as few or as many objects as you need to fulfill the earlier directives. trust downstream steps to resolve their concrete coordinates well regardless of how many pieces you output as a result of splitting. there are no exceptions to this rule.
</OBJECT_SPLITTING_GUIDANCE>

do NOT generate intangible ephemera (e.g. gasses, nebula clouds, etc.). moreover, when considering collections of objects placed in a certain shape or manner, always split it into its individual component objects, and use their placement texts and relationship arrays to manually construct the intended shape you have in mind.

for each object you output, carefully consider how you want to convey their spatial placement in the scene to the downstream step that converts your purely semantic descriptions into actual numeric coordinates and orientation. each object's placement string should be used to semantically describe where and how the object is sitting in the scene, while each object's orientation semantically describes what other objects or regions it is facing. each object's relationships array describes its relative positioning to other objects/regions where the placement text might not capture. all three of these fields come together to help the coordinate solver precisely calculate the spatial positioning of each object, so use them effectively to convey how exactly you want the objects to be laid out. none of these should contain any specific numbers or coordinates - they are purely semantic descriptions.
