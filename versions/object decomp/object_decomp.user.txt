the downstream generator builds one object at a time as a single, solid mesh grown from one reference image, so it can only ever produce something that reads as a single coherent physical body. take ownership over the geometry: walk the proposed objects and, for each one, decide whether it is one such body or whether it is really an assembly standing in for several. an object needs to be split whenever the generator could not convincingly produce it in one shot, or whenever the rest of the pipeline needs its internal structure to be explicit geometry it can build against later. the recurring cases are:

- container objects — things whose whole purpose is to hold, shelve, or present other objects that get placed on or in them later (a bookshelf, a wine rack, a shoe rack, a run of kitchen counter, a display cabinet, an open crate). generated as one opaque block, their shelves, compartments, and support surfaces are lost, and nothing can be placed into them afterwards. rebuild them from their real parts — uprights, shelves, backing, worktop, frame — so those surfaces become actual, addressable geometry.
- collections and groups — a single entry that in truth names many separate items (a stack of books, a bundle of firewood, a row of bollards, a scattering of pebbles, a full place setting). split it into its individual members and use each member's placement and relationships to recompose the arrangement the entry described.
- surfaces with openings — a wall carrying a doorway or window, a fence with a gate, a floor with a hatch. the generator cannot cut a clean, correctly positioned hole in a solid surface, so the surround has to be built as separate pieces around the gap.
- surfaces that must meet an uneven run of neighbours — a single surface stretched across neighbours that stop at different depths, sit at different heights, or push out to different extents cannot lie flush against all of them at once; break it into the sections each neighbour needs so every section can sit flush.

when you split an object, each resulting piece becomes its own object spec: give it a `prompt` that describes it as a standalone thing the generator can build on its own, and its own `placement`, `orientation`, and `relationships` so the pieces reassemble into exactly what the original object was. crucially, keep the whole set internally consistent — anything that referenced the object you split, whether as a structural `parent` or inside a `relationships` entry, and whether it is one of these proposed objects or already placed in the scene, must be re-pointed onto whichever piece it actually belongs to (an item that rested "on the bookshelf" now rests on a specific shelf). no field you emit may name an id that no longer exists after your split.

when an object slots something into a surface — a door leaf into a wall, a pane into a window band, or nothing at all (a bare gap is a valid thing to leave) — treat the surface as a full plane with both a length and a height, and treat the opening as a rectangle sitting somewhere within it. whatever fills the opening covers only its own extent, so the material that survives has to wrap the opening on every side it does not reach: the flanks to either side, the span above its head, and, when the opening lifts off the floor, the section beneath its sill. each surviving piece of that surround is its own object. before you consider a surface finished, look at everything slotted into it and ask what closes the plane around each one — if a piece is shorter or narrower than the surface it sits in, the surface is not yet complete.

resist the urge to over-decompose. an object the generator can already make as one believable mesh — a chair, a boulder, a lamp, a tree, a parked car — must be carried through exactly as proposed, id and fields intact; fracturing it into legs and a seat only shatters something that would have come out whole and clean. the test is never "could this be broken down further" (almost anything can), it is "will the generator fail to produce this as one body, or does the rest of the pipeline need its internal parts". the number of objects you return is neither a target to hit nor one to minimise — return as few or as many as the geometry honestly requires, and trust the downstream solver to place however many pieces you emit.
